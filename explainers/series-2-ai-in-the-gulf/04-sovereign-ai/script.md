[On-Screen Text: The chips. The cloud. The permission. Whose are they?]

Every Gulf state says it wants its own AI. Yet the chips, the cloud, and often the permission to use them come from Washington. By the end, you'll understand what sovereign AI really means, and who holds the off-switch.

[Pause 2s]

[Section: What sovereign AI means]

First, what the phrase actually means.

The term was popularised by a chipmaker. NVIDIA defines sovereign AI as "a nation's domestic capability to produce artificial intelligence using its own infrastructure, data, workforce, and business networks."

[On-Screen Text: NVIDIA: "a nation's domestic capability to produce artificial intelligence"]

I'm Wissam, and this is AI This Week, AI Explained.

[Pause]

NVIDIA says the goal is to protect local languages, values, culture and history. Its CEO, Jensen Huang, puts it more bluntly.

[On-Screen Text: Jensen Huang: "AI, like electricity and internet, is essential infrastructure for every nation."]

To see what "your own AI" involves, picture AI as a stack of layers. At the bottom are chips, the specialised processors called GPUs that do the heavy maths. Above them sit data centres, the buildings full of those chips.

[Pause]

Then comes the cloud, the service that rents that computing out. Then the models, the AI systems themselves. Then the data they learn from. And finally, the people who build all of it.

[On-Screen Text: The AI stack: chips → data centres → cloud → models → data → people]

Here's an everyday way to picture it. Think of owning a restaurant. You can own the building. You can write your own menu, in your own language.

[Pause]

But suppose the ovens come from one supplier. That supplier decides which chefs may use them and can inspect your kitchen. Then you own the restaurant, but not fully the cooking.

[Pause]

That is why the think tanks treat sovereignty as a spectrum, not an on-off switch. Brookings calls it a range of strategies for making independent decisions about AI, "rather than literal autarky." Autarky means total self-sufficiency. Carnegie also describes sovereignty as a spectrum, layer by layer.

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: Tony Blair: "No state can dominate every layer of the AI stack."]

The Tony Blair Institute says it "is about strategic agency, not self-sufficiency." A Gulf critic, Muhanad Seloom, offers the sharpest test. Sovereignty, he writes, is "the capacity to make consequential choices without another party's permission."

[Section: Why every country wants it]

So why is everyone chasing it?

Brookings lists four motives. They are national security, economic competitiveness, getting your language and culture into AI, and influence over global AI rules. Carnegie says these share one underlying worry: dependence.

The numbers show why.

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: US + China: more than 90% of global AI data-centre capacity (TBI, Jan 2026)]

The Tony Blair Institute said in early 2026 that the US and China control more than ninety per cent of global AI data-centre capacity.

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: NVIDIA GPUs in 52% of tracked AI projects (Carnegie, citing CNAS)]

And Carnegie, citing a report by CNAS, a Washington security think tank, says NVIDIA alone supplied the chips for fifty-two per cent of tracked AI infrastructure projects worldwide.

[Pause]

Language matters too. NVIDIA argues that local models trained on local data can reflect regional dialects and cultural context. For Arabic speakers, that is not a small thing.

[Pause]

The Gulf also has a natural edge. The Tony Blair Institute names the UAE and Saudi Arabia among countries with structural energy advantages for AI. AI runs on electricity, and lots of it.

[Section: Three Gulf playbooks]

Now, how three Gulf states are actually doing it.

Start with the UAE. WAM, the UAE's state news agency, reports it appointed the world's first federal Minister of Artificial Intelligence in 2017. It founded the university MBZUAI in 2019. Its national strategy aims to make it a leading AI nation by 2031.

[Pause]

In April 2024, Microsoft invested one and a half billion dollars in G42, Abu Dhabi's AI champion. G42 agreed to run its AI on Microsoft's Azure cloud. The deal sits under an agreement developed with both the UAE and US governments. CNBC reported that it followed months of US scrutiny of G42's alleged China ties. G42 "categorically" denied them.

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: UAE-US AI Campus, Abu Dhabi: 5GW (unveiled May 2025)]

In May 2025, a five-gigawatt UAE-US AI Campus was unveiled in Abu Dhabi. A gigawatt is a unit of electrical power. Here, it signals how much computing a site can run. WAM reports its compute is reserved for US hyperscalers, the giant cloud providers, and other approved providers.

[Pause]

Inside it sits Stargate UAE, a one-gigawatt cluster built by G42 and operated by OpenAI and Oracle. In May 2025, G42 said it expected the first 200 megawatts to go live in 2026.

[Pause]

The UAE has also released open-weight Arabic-capable models: Falcon, Jais and K2 Think. Open-weight means anyone can download and run them.

[Pause]

Saudi Arabia took a different route. Its 2020 national strategy set targets for 2030. One of them: about 15,000 local data and AI specialists. In May 2025, the Crown Prince launched HUMAIN, owned by the Public Investment Fund. PIF says HUMAIN builds "the entire AI stack," and supports Vision 2030 "by reducing reliance on oil."

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: HUMAIN: up to 500MW of AI factories | first phase: 18,000 GB300 chips]

NVIDIA and HUMAIN announced plans for up to 500 megawatts of "AI factories," meaning large AI data centres. The first phase is a supercomputer with 18,000 of NVIDIA's top GB300 chips. HUMAIN's Arabic model, ALLaM, was co-developed with Saudi Arabia's data and AI authority.

[Pause]

Qatar started later. Middle East AI News wrote that it "has been slower to invest" than its neighbours. In December 2025, it launched Qai, a national AI company under the Qatar Investment Authority.

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: Brookfield + Qai: $20 billion AI infrastructure partnership (Dec 2025)]

That same month, Brookfield and Qai announced a twenty-billion-dollar AI infrastructure joint venture. Qatar's own Arabic model, Fanar, launched in December 2024. It was trained on 1.3 trillion tokens, small chunks of text, and forty per cent of them were Arabic.

[Section: The gatekeeper: US export controls]

Next, the rulebook that decides who gets the chips.

Export controls are rules on which countries may buy sensitive technology. In January 2025, the Biden administration issued the AI Diffusion Rule. It sorted countries into three tiers. Many, in the middle tier, would have faced chip limits for the first time.

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: Timeline: Jan 2025 Diffusion Rule → May 2025 not enforced → Nov 2025 chip approvals → Jul 2026 UAE upgrade → Nov 2026 rule formally rescinded]

It never took effect. In May 2025, the Commerce Department told officials not to enforce it. Instead came country-by-country deals.

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: Up to 35,000 GB300 systems each for HUMAIN and G42 (Nov 2025)]

In November 2025, the data-centre trade publication DCD reported, citing the Wall Street Journal, that the US approved up to 35,000 GB300 systems each for HUMAIN and G42. Commerce said approvals were conditioned on "rigorous security and reporting requirements." It did not say what those were.

[Pause]

In July 2026, the US upgraded the UAE's export status, citing its Major Defense Partner role. UAE government agencies and approved companies can receive advanced chips without a licence. That list includes eight US firms, among them Microsoft and OpenAI. G42 and its sister company Core42 got 270 days. After that, they must "become US companies" or reapply.

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: G42 / Core42 approval deadline: 6 April 2027 (under the July 2026 rule)]

Then, in November 2026, a new US rule formally rescinded the Diffusion Rule. Instead of tiers, it controls specific destinations and data centres selling large amounts of compute to China-headquartered firms. According to EY, the UAE gets a licence requirement with a presumption of approval, plus its approved-entity carve-out.

[Pause]

The Tony Blair Institute sums up the bargain. The UAE gets "secure access to US frontier compute by accepting export controls and limits on Chinese collaboration."

[Section: Sovereign, or rented?]

Finally, the hard question: is this sovereignty at all?

Brookings warns that sovereign AI can become "a vehicle for protectionism," fragmented standards, and "stranded public investment." It also warns it can become a tool of "digital authoritarianism."

The IISS, a London defence think tank, says Gulf AI deals create dependencies on the United States that "contradict these autonomy claims." Carnegie says the UAE's turn away from China has deepened its reliance on Washington "in lieu of Beijing."

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: Muhanad Seloom: "who holds the off-switch"]

Seloom is blunter. "The chips are American. The cloud that runs them is, in most of these deals, operated by American firms." The real question, he says, is "who holds the off-switch."

[Pause]

Then there is physical risk. In early 2026, the IISS says, Iranian missiles struck the Gulf. Abu Dhabi's planned campus "sat directly in the strike zone."

[Pause]

CSIS, a Washington think tank, reports drone strikes on three Amazon Web Services data centres, two in the UAE and one in Bahrain. CSIS adds that this infrastructure also processed US military targeting data. "Gulf states sought data sovereignty," it writes; "what they got was closer to its inverse."

[Pause]

And people. The Atlantic Council names "continued reliance on expatriate talent." Its UAE report was supported by Google, and its centre receives UAE Embassy support. Seloom calls the engineer shortage the region's real constraint.

[Pause]

[On-Screen Text: Wissam's view]

[DRAFT VIEW: founder to confirm]

[Pause]

This part is my view, from twenty-five years selling software across this region. I don't think "rented" is an insult. Every enterprise I've worked with rents most of its stack. Nobody builds their own database engine. The serious question is the one every CIO asks a vendor: what happens if this relationship ends?

[Pause]

On chips and frontier models, the honest answer for the Gulf today is dependence. But Seloom himself points to the layers that can be owned: data, open-weight Arabic models, and people. That is where I'd watch for real sovereignty. The building is impressive. The team that knows how to cook is what lasts.

[Pause]

That is what Brookings, Carnegie and the Tony Blair Institute all call "managed interdependence." Own what's critical. Steer the rest. Depend, but with more than one door.

[Section: The bottom line]

Three things to remember.

First, sovereign AI is a spectrum, not total self-sufficiency. As Brookings put it, it's about independent decisions, not autarky.

Second, the UAE, Saudi Arabia and Qatar each have a national champion, big compute plans, and Arabic models. Their routes differ.

[Pause]

Third, the chips come with conditions written in Washington. That is why the real test is the off-switch: which layers you control, and which you only rent.
