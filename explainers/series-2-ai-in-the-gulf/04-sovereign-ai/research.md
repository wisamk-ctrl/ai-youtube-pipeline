## Facts

### What "sovereign AI" means
1. NVIDIA defines sovereign AI as "a nation's domestic capability to produce artificial intelligence using its own infrastructure, data, workforce, and business networks", with the goal "to protect and preserve local languages, values, culture, and history." [01]
2. NVIDIA lists five ingredients of a national AI strategy: an AI imperative, an AI-ready workforce, models and data "hosted and run on local infrastructure, subject only to local laws," a local ecosystem, and "AI factories", meaning locally owned, operated and governed AI clouds. [02]
3. Jensen Huang, NVIDIA's CEO: "AI, like electricity and internet, is essential infrastructure for every nation." (said at the HUMAIN launch, May 2025). [11]
4. Brookings defines AI sovereignty as "a spectrum of strategies to enhance a country's capacity to make independent decisions about critical AI infrastructure deployment, use, and adoption, rather than literal autarky." [04]
5. Carnegie: sovereign AI "generally refers to a nation's ability to independently design, develop, and govern AI systems using its domestic infrastructure, nationally held data, and a homegrown talent base," and analysts increasingly treat it as a spectrum across the stack: chips, cloud, data centres, models. [03]
6. The Tony Blair Institute (Jan 2026) says AI sovereignty "is about strategic agency, not self-sufficiency," and proposes a "Control, Steer, Depend" framework: control critical systems such as sensitive data, steer through regulation, procurement and standards, and depend on others in negotiated, diversified ways where there is no choice. [05][06]
7. Tony Blair: "No state can dominate every layer of the AI stack." [06]
8. Brookings' central finding: "full-stack AI sovereignty is structurally infeasible for almost any country." Brookings, Carnegie and TBI all recommend "managed interdependence" instead. [04][03][05]
9. A Gulf critic's definition: sovereignty is "the capacity to make consequential choices without another party's permission" (Muhanad Seloom, Middle East Council on Global Affairs). [25]

### Why countries pursue it
10. Brookings lists the motivations as national security and resilience, economic competitiveness, cultural and linguistic inclusion in training data, and influence in global AI governance. [04]
11. Carnegie names national security (keeping sensitive national data from foreign control), economic competitiveness and cultural/linguistic drivers, and says they share one underlying worry: dependence. [03]
12. TBI says the US and China "together control more than 90 per cent of global AI data-centre capacity." [05]
13. Carnegie, citing a CNAS report, says NVIDIA alone supplied the GPUs for 52 percent of all tracked AI infrastructure projects worldwide. [03]
14. NVIDIA says local foundation models trained on local data help "reflect regional dialects, cultural context and specific domains." [02]
15. TBI names Norway, the UAE, Saudi Arabia and France as having structural energy advantages for AI (solar or hydro potential, nuclear, strong grids, cheap capital). [05]

### How the UAE pursues it
16. The UAE's National Strategy for AI 2031 aims to make it one of the leading AI nations by 2031, "generating up to AED 335 billion in extra growth," and sets eight strategic objectives. [30]
17. The UAE appointed the world's first federal Minister of Artificial Intelligence in 2017 and founded MBZUAI in 2019, WAM reports. [09]
18. In April 2024 Microsoft invested $1.5 billion in G42, under an Intergovernmental Assurance Agreement developed "in close consultation with both the UAE and US governments." G42 agreed to run its AI on Microsoft Azure. [20]
19. CNBC reported that the deal followed months of US scrutiny of G42's alleged China ties. G42 "categorically" denied the allegations and said that since 2022 it had pursued a strategy "not to engage with Chinese companies." [21]
20. In May 2025 a 5GW UAE-US AI Campus was unveiled in Abu Dhabi, described as the largest outside the US. Its compute is "reserved for US hyperscalers and approved cloud service providers," and the two governments will "work together to regulate access." [09]
21. Stargate UAE is a 1GW cluster inside that campus, built by G42 and operated by OpenAI and Oracle with NVIDIA GB300 systems. G42 said the first 200MW was expected to go live in 2026. The campus covers 10 square miles. [10]
22. Carnegie says the UAE has "reportedly spent" $148 billion on AI investments at home and abroad since the start of 2024, and that economic diversification away from hydrocarbons is a key motive. [03]
23. TBI describes the UAE's posture as "frontier adjacency through accelerated state investment". It keeps direct control where AI meets national security and data governance, but "remains dependent on foreign chips and frontier models." [05]
24. The UAE has released open-weight Arabic-capable models: Falcon, Jais and K2 Think. [05][03]

### How Saudi Arabia pursues it
25. Saudi Arabia's National Strategy for Data & AI (2020) set 2030 targets: about 40% of the workforce trained in basic data and AI literacy, about 15,000 local specialists, about SAR 30bn of cumulative FDI and SAR 45bn of local investment in data and AI, and about 300 startups. [31]
26. On 12 May 2025 Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman launched HUMAIN, a PIF-owned company that he chairs and that operates "across the AI value chain." [12]
27. PIF says HUMAIN builds "the entire AI stack: data centers, cloud infrastructure, models, and applications" and supports Vision 2030 "by reducing reliance on oil". Its Arabic model ALLaM was co-developed with SDAIA. [13]
28. NVIDIA and HUMAIN plan up to 500MW of AI factories, powered by "several hundred thousand" NVIDIA GPUs over five years. The first phase is an 18,000-chip GB300 supercomputer. [11]
29. PIF says the 2024 Global AI Index ranked Saudi Arabia first in the world for government AI strategy. [12]

### How Qatar pursues it
30. In December 2025 Qatar launched Qai, a national AI company under the Qatar Investment Authority, to "develop, manage and invest in advanced AI infrastructures" in Qatar and abroad, in line with Qatar National Vision 2030. [23][22]
31. Brookfield and Qai announced a $20 billion AI-infrastructure joint venture on 9 December 2025, Brookfield's first AI infrastructure investment in the Middle East. [22]
32. Fanar, Qatar's sovereign Arabic LLM from QCRI at Hamad Bin Khalifa University, launched in December 2024. It was trained on 1.3 trillion tokens, 40% of them Arabic. Fanar 2.0 launched in December 2025. [23][24]
33. Middle East AI News wrote that Qatar "has been slower to invest" in far-reaching AI initiatives than its neighbours. [23]

### US export controls: the gatekeeper
34. The Biden administration issued the AI Diffusion Rule on 15 January 2025, with compliance due on 15 May 2025. It sorted countries into three tiers, and Tier 2 countries would have faced chip export limits for the first time. [07][08]
35. On 13 May 2025 Commerce told enforcement officials not to enforce the rule. It said the rule would have downgraded "dozens of countries" to "second-tier status" and promised a replacement. [07]
36. On 14 July 2026 the BIS head told Congress the administration no longer intends to replace the Diffusion Rule, Morgan Lewis reports. [17]
37. The US formally rescinded the Diffusion Rule in an interim final rule issued on 16 November 2026, effective 19 November. Instead of tiers, it uses destination-based controls (new licence requirements for Brazil, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand) and controls on data centres anywhere that sell large amounts of compute to China-headquartered companies. [18][19]
38. The same rule revokes the Biden-era controls on AI model weights. [19]
39. In November 2025 the US approved sales of up to 35,000 Nvidia GB300 systems (or equivalent) each to HUMAIN and G42, DCD reports, citing the WSJ. Commerce said approvals were "conditioned on both companies meeting rigorous security and reporting requirements." [14]
40. The investments alongside: Saudi Arabia raised its US investment pledges to almost $1 trillion, and the UAE pledged $1.4 trillion over ten years. [14]
41. In July 2026 BIS moved the UAE out of Country Groups D:3/D:4 and into A:5, citing its Major Defense Partner status. It lets the UAE government and approved companies receive advanced AI chips without a licence. [15]
42. Those Approved Entities are UAE government agencies; G42 and Core42 for 270 days, after which they must "become U.S. companies" or reapply; and eight US firms: Amazon, Apple, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Oracle and xAI. MGX only gets favourable licence review. [16]
43. TBI says the UAE-US AI Acceleration Partnership gives the UAE "secure access to US frontier compute by accepting export controls and limits on Chinese collaboration." [05]

### Criticisms and risks
44. Brookings warns that sovereign AI "can also become a vehicle for protectionism, fragmented markets and standards, and duplicative or stranded public investment," and can become a tool of "digital authoritarianism." [04]
45. IISS says Gulf AI deals embed "jurisdictional, technological and security-related dependencies on the United States that contradict these autonomy claims." [28]
46. Seloom: "The chips are American. The cloud that runs them is, in most of these deals, operated by American firms. The frontier models... are American". The real question, he says, is "who holds the off-switch." [25]
47. Carnegie: the UAE's turn away from China has "deepened the UAE's reliance on Washington in lieu of Beijing." [03]
48. War risk: IISS says Iranian ballistic missiles struck the Gulf in February 2026 and that Abu Dhabi's 5GW campus "sat directly in the strike zone." Brookings and CSIS report Iranian drone strikes on three AWS data centres, two in the UAE and one in Bahrain. [28][27][26]
49. CSIS: the same AWS infrastructure hosting Gulf banking was "simultaneously processing targeting data for U.S. military operations". "Gulf states sought data sovereignty; what they got was closer to its inverse." [26]
50. The Atlantic Council names the UAE's weak points as "continued reliance on expatriate talent," "incipient regulatory coherence," the need for stronger ethical safeguards, and pressure on energy and water. Seloom also names the shortage of engineers as the region's real constraint. [29][25]

## Conflicts and dates

- **HUMAIN chip scale.** NVIDIA (May 2025) said "several hundred thousand" GPUs over five years [11]. DCD (Nov 2025) says HUMAIN intends up to 600,000 Nvidia chips over three years [14]. CSIS (Jul 2026) says Nvidia was supplying "up to 600,000 GPUs" [26]. The newer figures are higher, but they are HUMAIN's stated intent, not deliveries. The US approval covers 35,000 GB300s [14].
- **The war: dates and targets.** IISS says ballistic missiles struck in February 2026 [28]. Brookings dates the AWS drone damage to March 1 [27]. These can both be true (missiles first, drones on AWS later). CSIS says "after the February strikes" [26]. On air, say "early 2026" unless the exact date matters.
- **"Replacement rule".** In May 2025 BIS promised a replacement [07]. In July 2026 the BIS head said there would be no replacement [17]. In November 2026 BIS issued a new remote-access rule that formally rescinds the old one [18]. The newest position is the November 2026 rule. Present it as "rescinded and replaced by a different approach," not "replaced".
- **"AI" vs "SI".** EY reports that under EO 14434 the November 2026 rule replaces "AI" with "Super Intelligence (SI)" throughout the export regulations [18]. The BIS May 2025 press release, fetched today, also reads "SI chips", while TechCrunch's contemporaneous quote of the same Kessler statement says "AI" [07][08]. On screen, quote the original wording and explain the rename only if useful.
- **Gulf investment totals vary by source and scope.** Carnegie: the UAE reportedly spent $148bn on AI since early 2024 [03]. DCD: UAE $1.4tn over ten years and Saudi ~$1tn in US investment pledges, across all sectors [14]. CSIS: ~$2.5tn of US-linked tech commitments by the three states [26]. These are not comparable figures; never add them up.
- **G42/Core42 approval has a deadline.** It expires on 6 April 2027 unless they "become US companies" or are re-approved [17][16]. This is very likely to change within the episode's shelf life.
- **The Stargate UAE "first 200MW in 2026" target** [10] is a May 2025 projection. Check whether it went live before the episode airs.
- **Saudi Arabia's export-control status** after the November 2026 rule was not stated in the sources fetched (the UAE's was). Do not describe it.
- **Think-tank funding.** The Brookings data-centre paper is funded by Google.org [27]. Atlantic Council GeoTech gets UAE Embassy support and Google funded the UAE report [29]. Mention this if those sources are quoted as neutral judges.

**Analogy for the hardest idea (the layers of sovereignty):** Owning a restaurant. You can own the building (data centres) and write your own menu in your own language (Arabic models). Sovereign AI is about choosing which parts of the kitchen you must own, and which you can safely rent.
